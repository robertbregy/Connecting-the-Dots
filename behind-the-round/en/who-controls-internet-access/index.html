Internet architecture · Governance explainer

Who controls access to the Internet?

A website can exist in the global DNS and still be unreachable from one network or country. Follow a request to see where control may be exercised, and why ICANN is not the Internet's universal censorship authority.

The distinction

DNS names, TLDs, registry contracts, national network filters and platform content decisions are separate layers. They can affect the same website, but they operate differently.

The registry and registrar manage the domain's registration and authoritative DNS status. They are not contacted as an extra network hop every time someone opens a website.

The resolver changes the answer

A local resolver may refuse the lookup or return a blocking address. Authoritative DNS and the TLD can still work normally elsewhere. An alternate resolver only helps if this is the sole relevant restriction.

What happens to the global name?The name may still be globally delegated.
What can users reach?Users of the affected resolver may not reach the target by its name.
Who might enforce this?A network operator, sometimes under a legal order.
[1][8]
Access interrupted in this scenario

The DNS name works. The route does not.

Even a valid DNS answer is insufficient when a network blocks access to the destination IP address or relevant route. A shared IP can host unrelated sites, creating collateral effects.

What happens to the global name?The name and DNS answer may be intact.
What can users reach?Connections to the blocked destination fail on that network.
Who might enforce this?An ISP or another network operator.
[1]
Access interrupted in this scenario

The secure connection fails

A filter can interrupt a connection during the TLS handshake, for example using visible server-name metadata when present. DNS can be correct and the server operational. Encryption details and countermeasures evolve.

What happens to the global name?The domain can remain normally resolved.
What can users reach?The HTTPS connection may reset or time out.
Who might enforce this?Network filtering equipment operated by providers or intermediaries.
[1][6]
Access interrupted in this scenario

The server or platform refuses the content

An application can remove a page, restrict an account, or geo-block content. The DNS and network can still function perfectly; this is not an ICANN action.

What happens to the global name?The domain can remain registered and resolved.
What can users reach?The requested service or content is unavailable under the platform's rules.
Who might enforce this?Hosting providers, applications or platform operators.
[1]
Access interrupted in this scenario

The domain's registration or delegation changes

Registry or registrar procedures may suspend a domain. Depending on the action, authoritative DNS may no longer publish the same delegation. This is not equivalent to deleting the entire TLD.

What happens to the global name?This individual registration/delegation may cease working globally.
What can users reach?Reachability may be affected across many networks.
Who might enforce this?Registries and registrars within applicable procedures and contracts.
[4]
Access interrupted in this scenario

Connectivity is cut for a population

A shutdown interrupts Internet communications for a region or group. It is not a DNS or TLD deletion. Its scope can differ between mobile and fixed networks and need not be technically total.

What happens to the global name?Global DNS names are not necessarily changed.
What can users reach?Broad groups of services become unavailable from the affected network.
Who might enforce this?Network operators, potentially acting under government orders.
[2][3]

The registry step describes control over registration and authoritative delegation, not an additional network hop on each visit. The exact outcome depends on how the measure is implemented.

Who controls what?

The same incident can involve a legal decision, a network-level enforcement action and an unrelated global DNS state. Do not confuse the actor with the technical layer.

ICANN / IANA

Coordinate names and the root-zone framework; they do not generally order national ISPs to block websites. The 2022 Ukraine letter illustrates the limits of this mandate.

[4]

Registry / registrar

Govern registrations and contractual suspensions under the rules that apply to a TLD. Actions affecting a registration are not the same as country-level filtering.

[4]

Government / authority

May create legal obligations within its jurisdiction. The scope, safeguards and justification depend on the law and on the measure.

[8][9]

Access provider

May implement DNS, IP, traffic or routing restrictions; different networks in the same country can behave differently.

[1][8]

Platform / hosting

May moderate content, refuse service or apply territorial restrictions without changing DNS delegation.

[1]

Five documented examples, five different contexts

A curated historical selection with dated evidence, not a complete or current inventory of countries that restrict online access.

2023

China · DNS injection plus TLS interference

OONI documented that access to its own website was disrupted in China by injected DNS answers and interference during TLS connections. This observation concerns the tested services and networks, not an assertion about every website in China.

OONI study published 2023

[5]
2023–2024

Russia · DNS and traffic-level filtering

In measurements from September 2023 to September 2024, OONI and RKS Global documented blocking of independent media. TLS handshake interference predominated in the studied sample, alongside some DNS-based blocks.

OONI / RKS Global, December 2024

[6]
June 2025

Iran · suspected app blocking and connectivity disruption

Between 15 June and 12 July 2025, OONI observed repeated anomalies consistent with possible WhatsApp blocking on some Iranian networks, without establishing that it affected every network. During the June conflict, OONI and other monitoring systems recorded evidence of severe connectivity disruption. App-level interference and a network shutdown remain distinct findings.

OONI findings, June–July 2025

[7][3]
2026

Switzerland · legally mandated DNS blocking

Gespa and the Federal Gaming Board maintain lists of unlicensed foreign gambling offers. Swiss access providers must apply DNS blocks for the listed domains. The measure is domestic; it does not remove those TLDs from the global root.

Gespa official blocklist, August 2026

[8]
2017

Catalonia (Spain) · domain actions and network blocking

During the 2017 referendum, OONI measured DNS tampering and HTTP blocking of referendum sites. Some .cat domains were also redirected at the DNS level after Fundació .cat implemented a court order, while ISPs applied other blocks. Actions affecting particular registrations are not removal of the .cat TLD: it is a sponsored gTLD, not a ccTLD.

OONI measurements, September–October 2017

[9]

Measurements are evidence, not an automatic verdict

OONI distinguishes automatically confirmed blocks (for example known block pages or known DNS censorship addresses) from anomalies that can have benign causes. Repeated results on identified networks and dates strengthen an interpretation; one failure cannot prove censorship. Internet Society Pulse separately records documented connectivity disruptions under its own inclusion methodology. Neither dataset is a universal real-time score for all countries.
