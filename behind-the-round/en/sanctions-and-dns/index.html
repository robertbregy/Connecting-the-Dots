Governance explainer · OFAC & DNS

When a global Internet meets national sanctions

ICANN coordinates a global naming system, yet must obey the laws that apply to it. That creates constraints on who can obtain a new gTLD, but does not give ICANN a general power to disconnect countries from the DNS.

Legal compliance

ICANN may be barred from providing services to certain applicants unless an authorization or exemption applies.

Technical neutrality

ICANN says its mandate is not to impose geopolitical sanctions or unilaterally disconnect country-code domains.

What the rules actually mean

Why US sanctions reach a global organization

ICANN is a California-based nonprofit. Its global mission does not exempt it from applicable US laws, including OFAC economic and trade sanctions. These laws govern certain dealings with people, entities and jurisdictions. They do not make every domain associated with a sanctioned country illegal.

[2][5]

New gTLD applications: designated persons are not the same as residents

For the 2026 round, ICANN explicitly says it cannot accept applications from individuals or entities on OFAC's Specially Designated Nationals (SDN) List. A separate question arises when an applicant is not listed but is located in a sanctioned jurisdiction: whether an exemption or US authorization permits ICANN to provide services. Neither nationality alone nor an individual's listing proves that a different organization is sanctioned.

[1][2]

Licenses matter. Approval is not automatic.

OFAC distinguishes general licenses, which authorize classes of transactions under stated conditions, from specific licenses granted case by case. ICANN says it has previously obtained licenses for applicants in sanctioned countries who were not individually designated. ICANN has no duty to apply in every case and OFAC is not obliged to grant a request. ICANN's community has also recommended examining general licenses to reduce recurring barriers.

[2][3][4][9]

Four different layers, four different consequences

An applicant for a new gTLD, a TLD registry operator, an accredited registrar and a customer registering a second-level domain are not interchangeable roles. Contracting, accreditation, sales and DNS delegation trigger different obligations and involve different actors. A blocked transaction with one actor does not automatically erase a ccTLD from the root zone or revoke every name registered under it.

[2][8]

Even 'sanctioned country' does not mean 'no domain names'

A concrete counterexample: OFAC's Cuba guidance says domain-name registration is among the Internet-communications services authorized in most circumstances under a relevant general license. This is not a universal exemption. It shows why the exact sanctions program, transaction, parties and authorization matter more than a country label.

[6][4]

OFAC is not the only sanctions framework

UN, EU, UK and national rules may also matter to actors within their respective jurisdiction or where legally applicable. Their scopes differ. ICANN's US obligations should not be conflated with a universal rule binding every non-US registrar or domain holder in precisely the same way.

[2][5]

What the 2026 evidence does not establish

ICANN reported 1,616 paid applications on 22 September and 1,615 at Reveal Day on 7 October. It has not publicly identified an OFAC exclusion as the reason for the change. The .wdo application has been subject to a separately documented administrative check, but its precise reason is not publicly established. Treat sanctions as relevant background, not a proposed explanation for either observation.

[10][11][12]

Four documented moments

These events belong to different legal and procedural categories. They are not four examples of OFAC removing TLDs.

2012

No sanctions-based rejections reported

ICANN states that no application in the 2012 new gTLD round was rejected because of its sanctions-compliance obligations. This does not mean that sanctions screening or licensing was absent.

[3]
2014

A court refused attachment of .ir, .sy and .kp

Judgment creditors sought to seize country-code TLDs associated with Iran, Syria and North Korea. A US federal court quashed the attempt. This was a civil enforcement dispute about attachable property, not ICANN rejecting a new gTLD application under OFAC rules.

[7]
2022

ICANN declined to disconnect Russian ccTLDs

Following Ukraine's request concerning .ru, .рф and .su, ICANN said its technical mandate did not extend to imposing sanctions or unilaterally cutting off national domains. It also noted that ICANN does not issue SSL certificates or control all root-server nodes.

[8]
2026

Eligibility and legal screening are explicit

The new Applicant Guidebook and associated FAQs make sanctions compliance part of the 2026 process and differentiate SDN-listed applicants from potentially licensable transactions involving other applicants.

[1][2]

Why this matters for the 2026 round

The difference between 1,616 paid applications announced on 22 September and the 1,615 reported at Reveal Day is a documented discrepancy, not proof of sanctions enforcement. The public administrative check involving .wdo also has no confirmed OFAC cause. Follow the two investigations separately.
